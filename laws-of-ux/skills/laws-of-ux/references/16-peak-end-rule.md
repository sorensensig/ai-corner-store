# Peak-End Rule

*Law `#16` in the Laws of UX collection [1]. Primary source: Kahneman et al. [18].
Synthesised, not quoted.*

## What it says

People judge an experience largely by how it felt at its most intense point and at its
end, rather than by the sum or average of the whole. Duration barely registers.

## Why it holds

Kahneman and colleagues found participants preferred a longer unpleasant experience with
a milder ending over a shorter one that ended at peak discomfort — objectively more
total pain, remembered as less [18]. The distinction is between the *experiencing self*
and the *remembering self*; only the latter decides whether to come back.

## What it constrains

- **Identify your peaks — both directions.** The most delightful and the most painful
  moments are what get remembered and recounted. Everything between them is
  approximately invisible.
- **Endings are disproportionate.** The last screen, the confirmation, the sign-out, the
  final line of output. A strong ending can redeem a mediocre middle; a bad ending
  discards a good one.
- **Beyond product flows:** an agent session is remembered by its worst moment and its
  last. A run that did excellent work and ended with a confusing error is remembered as
  a failure. This is why a clear closing summary is not politeness — it is the part that
  determines whether the whole thing counts as having worked.

## Getting it wrong

- Optimising average satisfaction across a flow while leaving one severe pain point
  intact. The average is not what is remembered.
- Manufacturing delight at the end of a genuinely bad experience. The peak is still
  there, and a cheerful ending on top of real frustration reads as tone-deaf.
- Forgetting that error states are peaks. They are the most intense moments most users
  will have, and they are usually the least designed.
