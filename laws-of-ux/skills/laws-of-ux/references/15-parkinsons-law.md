# Parkinson's Law

*Law `#15` in the Laws of UX collection [1]. Primary source: Parkinson [17].
Synthesised, not quoted.*

## What it says

Work expands to fill the time available for its completion. Give a task an hour and it
takes an hour; give it a day and it takes a day.

## Why it holds

Parkinson's satirical 1955 essay observed British civil service headcount growing
independently of the work to be done [17]. The mechanism is that available capacity
becomes an implicit target: without a binding constraint, scope, polish and deliberation
grow to consume whatever is there.

## What it constrains

- **Constrain the time, and the scope self-limits.** A short explicit budget produces a
  narrower, usually adequate solution — and reveals what was genuinely essential.
- **Anticipate to shorten perceived duration.** Autofill, smart defaults and predictive
  input finish a task in less time than the user allotted for it, which reads as
  competence.
- **Beyond process:** this is the governing law of agent sessions. Without a stopping
  condition, an agent will keep finding legitimate-looking work — more verification,
  more polish, one more refactor — until the budget is exhausted. The available context
  window and token budget function exactly as Parkinson's "time available". A session
  with no natural stopping point does not stop; it runs out.

## Getting it wrong

- Compressing timeframes without cutting scope, which produces the same work done badly
  rather than less work done well. The law is about scope discipline, not speed.
- Using it to justify permanently unrealistic deadlines. Parkinson describes an
  expansion tendency; it does not mean any deadline is achievable.
- Setting a budget without defining *done*. A time limit with no completion criterion
  just relocates the expansion into the final hour.
