# The talent myth

*Dysfunction `#95` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7501902750255841281-mNRm/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Hiring is built around finding motivated, self-driven people: the interview loop probes for drive, ownership and passion, and the offer goes to whoever shows most of it.
- The perks follow — flexible hours, a learning budget, "we trust you to get the job done" — and leadership genuinely means them.
- Eighteen months on, the engagement survey reads the same as it did before. The people who arrived burning either left, went quiet, or turned out to be no more driven than anyone else.
- The conclusion drawn is a hiring conclusion: the bar slipped, the screening missed something, we need a better calibre of person. So the loop gets another stage.
- Meanwhile the ones who stayed and still care are visibly paying for it, and are held up as proof that motivation is a personal quality after all.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The Agile Manifesto's fifth principle — build projects around motivated individuals, give them the environment and support they need — gets the outcome right and the mechanism backwards. It treats motivation as an input you recruit and then protect. In open sociotechnical systems theory it is an **output**: an emergent property of the structure someone works inside. The same person is motivated in one design and flat in another, and nothing about them changed.

What produces it is specified, not mysterious. Emery and Trist's **six psychological job requirements** name the conditions: room to make decisions about your own work; continual learning, which needs both the ability to set goals and timely feedback against them; enough variety to stay engaged; mutual support and respect from the people you work with; meaningfulness, which requires socially valued work *and* sight of how your part joins the whole; and a desirable future — a path that goes somewhere. These are properties of **job and group design**, not of biography. A **DP1** structure — redundancy of parts, each person holding a fragment, coordination sitting one level up — systematically withholds the first, the fifth and the sixth by construction. You can hire for drive all you like; the structure will convert it back to compliance at roughly the same rate every time.

This is why the survey does not move. The organisation is running a selection intervention against a design problem, so its own results keep confirming the wrong theory: individuals vary, therefore individuals are the variable. And the six requirements are **group-level** conditions — the whole task has to belong to a team that can actually decide about it, which is why enriching one role at a time (`#61`) leaves the numbers flat too. Under DP1 the motivated hire has two options: absorb the gap personally until they are tired, or stop. Both get read as a fact about them.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — stop recruiting motivation and start designing a structure that produces it in whoever happens to be there.**
- Make the group, not the individual, the unit that owns a whole task, with the authority to control variance where it arises. That is what puts the six requirements within reach at all.
- Audit job and team design directly against the six: decision latitude, learning with real feedback, variety, mutual support, visible contribution to a valued whole, a future worth having. Each gap is a design fault with a design remedy.
- Let the people doing the work redesign it — a Participative Design Workshop exists precisely because the six conditions cannot be granted from above without contradicting the first one.
- Judge the change by whether the conditions are now present, not by the next engagement score — the score is a lagging read on the structure.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- When low engagement is diagnosed as a hiring problem, ask the falsifying question: *which of the six does this job actually offer?* It reframes without accusing anyone, and it is answerable in the room.
- Within your own team you can move variety, mutual support and feedback quite far without permission — rotate work, pair, make the whole output visible to everyone who touched a piece of it.
- Be honest about the ceiling: decision latitude and a desirable future are usually set above you. Naming which two of the six you cannot supply is more useful than a morale push that implies the rest is willpower.
- Defend people from the character reading. Someone who has gone quiet in a structure that offers them none of the six is responding accurately, not failing.
