# We need a resource

*Dysfunction `#89` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7500084318317297664-8Rgx/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A request arrives in the shape of a unit: "we need two Java developers for three months, starting Monday." Not a problem to solve, not an outcome — a quantity of a labelled thing.
- Staffing is done by intersecting two lists: who has the label on their profile, and who is free. The architect who has spent five years on resilience in distributed systems has "Java" on his profile and open capacity, so he goes.
- The work turns out to be routine maintenance. He does it competently, because he would. Nothing visibly goes wrong, so nothing gets corrected.
- The one thing he actually knows better than almost anyone never comes up, and nobody on either side is in a position to notice that it didn't.
- Asked about it, everyone gives a defensible answer: the client asked for Java developers, he is a Java developer, he was available, he delivered. The system worked exactly as designed, which is the problem.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This is Emery's **redundancy of parts** applied to expertise. A person is reduced to one visible attribute — a technology tag on a CV — and then treated as one of many interchangeable units carrying that tag. Interchangeability is the whole point of the design: it is what makes a staffing spreadsheet work, what lets a request be answered on Friday for a Monday start, and what makes utilisation a number you can manage. But interchangeable parts have no distinctive knowledge by definition, so a system built to allocate them cannot see distinctive knowledge, and therefore cannot fail to use it. The loss never appears anywhere.

The missing thing is coordination, and it is missing structurally rather than accidentally. Matching competence to work is real work: something has to hold both pictures at once — what this person can actually do, and what this assignment actually needs — and connect them deliberately. In a DP1 arrangement that job sits above the work, in a staffing or resourcing function, and the only inputs it can get at that distance are labels and calendars. It is not that the staffing lead is careless; it is that the position they occupy cannot see the thing the decision requires. Meanwhile the two parties who *can* see it — the person with the expertise and the people living the problem — never meet before the assignment is fixed.

And the cost lands squarely on two of the **six psychological requirements for motivating work**: **variety** and **continual learning**. Both depend on being placed on work that is actually near what you know and want to get better at. Staff by label for long enough and the label becomes true: the specialism goes unused, then goes stale, then stops being what anyone asks for — and the organisation concludes it never had that capability, having spent years arranging not to use it. The word *resource* is not sloppy language here. It is an accurate description of what the structure has decided a person is.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — put the matching where the knowledge is, and stop trading in units.**
- Change what a request is allowed to say. Make the asking unit a *problem and its context* — what we're trying to do, what's hard about it, what's already been tried — rather than a count of labelled bodies. A request that names an outcome can be matched on capability; one that names "two Java developers" can only be matched on labels.
- Let the people who will do the work take part in the match. A short conversation between the candidate and the actual team, before assignment, surfaces in ten minutes what no profile encodes — and gives both sides the standing to say "wrong fit" while that is still cheap.
- Give a standing group ownership of its own composition and commitments, rather than having members allocated into it from outside. When the group that holds the work also decides who joins it and what it takes on, the matching happens at the point of work, continuously, with the real information present.
- Stop managing utilisation as the primary number. Individual billable occupancy is precisely the metric that makes label-and-availability staffing rational; while it is the measure, every improvement you try will be argued away as slack.
- Treat depth as something to be developed and deployed on purpose. If nobody's job includes knowing what your specialists are actually deep in, and steering work toward it, the depth is decorative.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Get to the requester before the assignment hardens. One question — "what's the problem behind the two developers?" — often reveals an assignment that a different person, or a different shape of engagement, would serve far better.
- Make your own expertise legible in the terms the system uses, since profiles are what it reads. That is a workaround, not a fix, and worth doing anyway: it is the cheapest way to stop being matched on your weakest true label.
- Named badly, this reads as bad luck or a bad staffing lead. Name it accurately instead — the structure has no channel through which competence can reach the work — and you get a conversation that can change something, rather than an apology.
- Where you can, carve a slice of the assignment that does use what you know, and make its value visible to the client. Evidence that the match mattered travels further than an argument that it should have.
