# The org chart nobody uses

*Dysfunction `#97` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7502055446799683584-Ry-Z/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A new hire is walked through the org chart in week one — development, operations, finance, product, each with its own manager and its own box. They never open it again.
- What they actually get taught, quietly, is the real map: which team to ask when you need infrastructure, which person in finance understands the product, which architect two divisions away has to be onside before anything big moves.
- Coordination lives in chat channels that cut straight across the chart. The channels have no owner, no mandate and no line on any diagram, and everything depends on them.
- Ask who decides something and you get a name, not a box — and the name rarely matches the box.
- Getting anything across the organisation takes a round of asking, favours and informal escalation that nobody logs as work, because on paper it does not exist.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

There are three organisations in the building at once: the formal one on the chart, the informal web of relationships that actually moves work through it, and the unit where the work is really done. When those three are different things, people are spending real effort bridging the distance between them — and that effort is invisible, so it is never counted, never resourced and never designed.

The split is produced by **how the boxes were drawn**. A **DP1** structure groups people by *input* — discipline, function, resource pool — because that is the convenient unit for managing headcount, hiring and utilisation. It is a coherent way to administer people. It is close to useless as a way to get a whole piece of work done, because no single box contains one. Delivering anything requires crossing four or five of them, and in DP1 the crossing points are exactly where no one has authority: each box has a manager above it, and integration is only available at the level above them all. So people route around the design. The informal network **is** the missing coordination, improvised by the people doing the work because the formal structure does not supply it. Variance that arises in the flow has to be controlled somewhere, and if the structure won't do it, relationships will.

Read that way, the informal network is not a culture win to celebrate or a shadow structure to stamp out — it is a **measurement of the gap** between how work is organised and how work actually happens. This is the oldest finding in the field: at Haighmoor the Tavistock researchers found the productive system was the one the miners had organised among themselves, not the one on the plan. A **DP2** structure closes the gap rather than bridging it: group by *output*, give a self-managing team a whole task with the capabilities it needs inside its own boundary, and the formal structure and the working structure become the same thing. There is then no gap for a workaround to fill. Note what this costs to ignore: an organisation whose real operating system is undocumented, unowned and resident in individual heads is one resignation away from losing a piece of it.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — draw the boxes around what gets delivered, not around what people are.**
- Start from whole tasks, not from disciplines. Identify the end-to-end pieces of work that produce something a customer or another unit actually receives, then form units around those, with the mix of skills each one needs inside its own boundary.
- Use the informal network as the design input. Map where the real coordination runs today; every strong informal link across a formal boundary marks a line that was drawn in the wrong place.
- Move authority with the work. Grouping by output changes nothing if decisions still climb to a functional manager — that reinstates the gap under a new chart.
- Let the people who live the handoffs do the redesign, rather than having a target operating model handed to them. They are the ones with the map.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Write the real map down. Make the working structure visible — who actually coordinates what, through which channel — so it stops being private knowledge and starts being evidence anyone with authority over the chart can read.
- Count the bridging. Track how long a typical cross-boundary request takes and how many people it touches. That converts "we have good relationships here" into a number that describes a design cost.
- Protect and share the connectors rather than relying on them. If two or three people are the reason the place works, that is a structural exposure, not a staffing success.
- When someone proposes fixing this with a new process, a liaison role or a coordination forum, name what it is: a fourth layer over the gap, not a closing of it.
