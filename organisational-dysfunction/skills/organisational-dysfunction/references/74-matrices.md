# Matrices

*Dysfunction `#74` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491965884832391168-8q_4) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A developer's engineering manager books training on Thursday; the product owner needs the feature by Friday. Neither of them spoke to the other, and the developer is left to decide which one to disappoint.
- Both lines are legitimate — one owns craft, career and professional standards, the other owns delivery and the customer — so there is no wrong answer available, only a wrong person to be annoyed with you.
- Priorities get "aligned" in a recurring meeting between the two managers that resolves the calendar but never the underlying claim on the same hours.
- Which work actually happens tracks who is more senior, more persistent, or closer to the person setting budgets — not which work matters more.
- Everyone can describe the matrix on a slide, and nobody can tell you who decides when the two lines disagree.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

A matrix is an attempt to run both design principles in the same structure at once. The functional line is **DP1**: it supplies control, a career ladder, and professional identity through a hierarchy above the work. The cross-functional line is meant to be **DP2**: a team owning a whole piece of delivery and coordinating it themselves. Each is coherent on its own. Together they are not, because they are answers to the same question — *where does responsibility for coordinating and controlling the work sit?* — and that question admits one answer per unit of work. The matrix doesn't integrate the two principles; it declares the contradiction resolved and pushes it downward, to be absorbed by whoever sits at the intersection.

Emery's name for the result is a **mini bureaucracy**. The intersection is a location in the structure where two lines of authority meet with no mechanism to settle between them, so it becomes a standing focus for inter-professional competition. What looks like a scheduling conflict is a boundary dispute between two managers whose mandates overlap, and neither can concede without losing ground they will need next quarter. The consequence is precise and worth naming: whether the right priority wins is secondary to which side wins the internal power game. Nobody has to be political for this to happen — the structure rewards the behaviour whatever the intentions of the people in it.

Notice where the variance ends up. The developer holds a genuine conflict, has full information about the trade-off, and possesses none of the authority to resolve it; the two managers hold the authority and see only their own half. That is variance control lifted away from the point of work, which is the DP1 signature, and it is why the fix is never better communication between the managers. Emery's own conclusion was that matrix coordination depends on a shared commitment strong enough to override professional self-interest — and that structure cannot manufacture such commitment. Dual reporting is a design that only works when it is not needed.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — pick one line of authority over the work and make the other advisory, rather than asking a person to arbitrate between two bosses.**
- Give the team a whole task and the authority to sequence it. Where the work is done is where the priority call belongs; both managers become inputs to that call, not competing claims on it.
- Keep the functional line, but change what it owns: professional standards, capability, craft development and long-term skill — not the allocation of this week's hours. Career structure and professional identity are real needs and do not require command over daily work to be met.
- Make the trade-off visible above the individual. If training and delivery genuinely compete, that is a capacity decision for the people who set capacity, and it should be resolved before it reaches a calendar.
- Be suspicious of "alignment" mechanisms — steering groups, priority councils, a RACI — added on top. They are the mini bureaucracy growing to manage a contradiction that would be cheaper to remove.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Stop resolving it privately. Put the conflict back where both mandates are visible: one message, both managers, the two commitments side by side, and a request for a decision rather than an apology. This is not escalation for its own sake — it returns a structural conflict to the level that owns it.
- Ask the team to hold the priority, not you. A conflict that lands on a person is a personal loyalty test; the same conflict held by a group is a scheduling problem with an owner.
- Name the pattern once, plainly, without indicting either manager. Both are behaving rationally inside their remit; the cost is real and it is being paid somewhere nobody is looking.
- Notice when you start choosing by who reacts worst. That is the power game selecting your work for you, and it is worth saying out loud before it becomes how you plan.
