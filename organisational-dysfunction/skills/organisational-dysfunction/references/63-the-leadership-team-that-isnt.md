# The leadership team that isn't

*Dysfunction `#63` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7489927427721302016-PFbN) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The "leadership team" meets every other week and the agenda is a lap of the table: engineering, product, finance, people, sales — each reporting on their own domain.
- Nobody in the room has any real stake in anyone else's item; attention drifts until it's your turn again.
- Anything that cuts across functions gets settled bilaterally afterwards — a Slack thread, a corridor chat, a one-to-one with the CEO — never in the meeting itself.
- The CEO becomes the integrator and therefore the bottleneck: cross-cutting decisions queue up behind their calendar.
- Everyone is individually competent and well-intentioned, which is exactly why it's confusing that the group achieves nothing as a group.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This group has the *form* of a **DP2** module and the *wiring* of **DP1**. In a genuine DP2 module, control over each specialist domain still sits with the individual who holds it — but responsibility for coordination and for the shared goals belongs to the group as a whole. That second half is what's missing here. Each member coordinates *upward* with the CEO rather than *sideways* with peers, so the CEO occupies the classic DP1 position: the level above the work, integrating fragments that were never designed to integrate themselves.

The structural reason is simpler than it looks: **the group has no goals that none of them can achieve alone.** A team is constituted by a shared task, not by a shared calendar slot. Give five function heads a set of objectives each can deliver inside their own silo and you have not built a team — you have built a status meeting, and peer coordination becomes optional overhead. People stop coordinating because there is nothing structurally requiring them to, and the moment they stop, the CEO has to step in, which confirms the reporting pattern and deepens it.

This is also how it decays over time. Strategic goals that once genuinely spanned functions go stale; nobody notices, because the group's day-to-day mode — report, listen, escalate — works just as well with no shared goal at all. The absence only shows up as symptoms: slow cross-cutting decisions, an overloaded CEO, and executives who quietly optimise their own function because that is the only thing they are actually held to.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — give the group a whole task and make coordination the group's job, not the CEO's.**
- Define a small set of live strategic goals that **no single member can deliver alone**, and make the group jointly responsible for them. Without that, nothing else on this list will hold.
- Move cross-functional decisions into the group as peer decisions, with the CEO participating rather than adjudicating — if the CEO is the tie-breaker, coordination will keep routing through them.
- Change the meeting from serial reporting to joint work on the shared goals: functional updates go in writing beforehand, and the time in the room is spent on what the goals require.
- Refresh those goals deliberately. Coordination breaking down is usually the signal that the shared goals have gone stale, not that the people have.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Take one cross-cutting problem you and a peer both own and work it as peers to a decision, then bring the decision — not the question — to the group. It's a small, legible demonstration that the coordination can happen at your level.
- When you're about to escalate something to the CEO, ask first whether it's actually a peer negotiation you're routing upward out of habit.
- Name the pattern without indicting anyone: the group's mandate is the problem, not its members' willingness. "What are we jointly on the hook for that none of us can do alone?" is a fair question to put on an agenda.
