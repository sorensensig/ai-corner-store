# Removing management

*Dysfunction `#86` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7501151188067713024--q-X/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A management layer is removed — usually the middle — in the name of agility, flatness, or cost. Teams are told they are now self-managing, effective immediately.
- The work those managers did does not leave with them. Prioritising between competing asks, chasing the thing stuck in another department, holding context nobody else holds, deciding when to escalate — it all still has to happen.
- Within weeks somebody in each team is doing it. No title, no mandate, no training, and no way for anyone to disagree with them formally.
- Decisions get slower rather than faster, because the thing that used to be one person's job is now nobody's and everybody's, and the team has no method for settling anything.
- Leadership reads the friction as a maturity problem — the teams "aren't ready for autonomy yet" — and either waits it out or quietly reappoints the layer under a new name.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Deleting a layer changes who is on the org chart. It does not change **where responsibility for coordinating and controlling the work sits**, and that is the only thing the design principle is about. Under DP1 those functions were held above the work by managers; remove the managers without relocating the functions and they do not vanish, they go unowned — and unowned functions migrate. They reappear as informal leaders, as whoever is most senior or most willing, as the person who talks to the stakeholder. The team has been handed the *consequences* of coordination without the *authority* or the *capability* for it.

What this produces is not DP2. OST is precise here: the absence of a design principle is its own condition — **laissez-faire**, where coordination is neither held above the work nor genuinely held within it. DP2 is a positive design. It requires a multiskilled group with a whole task, control over its own methods, and the capacity to set goals, absorb variance, and settle disagreement internally. None of that arrives by subtraction. Removing managers without redesigning the work produces an accidental laissez-faire and then blames the teams for how it feels.

The specific loss is **variance control at the point of work**. A manager was, whatever else, a mechanism: something that noticed when the plan and reality diverged and did something about it. Take the mechanism away and put nothing in its place and the variance still arrives — it just now gets absorbed by whoever happens to be standing there, without information, mandate, or any way to be held to it. That is worse than the hierarchy, and it is why the reappointment is so predictable. The organisation is not regressing out of nostalgia; it is restoring the only variance-control mechanism it ever built. The honest question is never whether to keep the layer, but **what will hold coordination, goal-setting and control once it is gone** — and that has to be designed and built before the layer comes out, not discovered afterwards.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — self-management is a capability you build into the group, not a layer you subtract from the chart.**
- Name the functions the layer actually performed before removing anyone: prioritisation, cross-boundary coordination, escalation, context, capacity, conflict resolution. Each one needs an explicit new home inside the group, with the authority to go with it.
- Give the group a **whole task with a measurable outcome** and control over its own methods. Self-management without a boundary you own is just being on your own.
- Build the capability deliberately — how the team decides, how it settles disagreement, how it sets and revises its own goals. This is skilled work and nobody arrives knowing it; the Norwegian industrial-democracy experiments treated it as a design and training programme, not an announcement.
- Redesign the surviving management levels too. If the level above still sets the objectives, grades the result, and owns the budget, the team is self-managing in method only — and the removed layer will be recreated to broker between them.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Make the informal leadership visible and explicit. If someone is doing the coordination anyway, the team deciding who does it and for how long is far healthier than it defaulting to whoever will not let it drop.
- Write down what the team is actually allowed to decide, get it confirmed once in writing, and work to it. Most of the paralysis after a de-layering is uncertainty about mandate rather than absence of it.
- Take one function at a time — start with prioritisation inside the team's own backlog — and build a working method for it before adopting the next. Trying to become self-managing all at once is how teams conclude they can't.
- Reframe the "not ready for autonomy" verdict when it arrives. Nothing was built; something was removed. That distinction lands with leadership in a way "we need more support" does not.
