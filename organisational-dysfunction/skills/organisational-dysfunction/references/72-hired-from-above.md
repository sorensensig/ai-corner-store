# Hired from above

*Dysfunction `#72` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7493907651685347329-79qn) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The team is behind. A manager procures a consultant or transfers someone in, and the team finds out at the standup where the new person is introduced.
- The new joiner needs environments, context, domain background, review time and someone to pair with — all of it drawn from the same people who were already the constraint.
- Two weeks in, the team is further behind than before the "help" arrived, and everybody is slightly embarrassed about saying so.
- Nobody can name what the new person is accountable for, because the work was never divided in a way that had a spare whole piece in it.
- When the engagement ends, the team's own account is that nothing much changed — the person was there, and never really joined.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Who joins a group is the most fundamental design decision that group can make, and this dysfunction is what it looks like when that decision sits somewhere else. Under **DP2**, a self-managing team controls its own composition: it decides what capability it is missing, who fits, and at what pace it can absorb someone, because those judgements are inseparable from owning a whole task. Under **DP1**, staffing is a management function performed above the work — a resource decision about interchangeable units. The consultant is that logic made visible. A group that cannot design its own membership cannot fully own its own work, whatever authority it has been granted on paper, because the boundary of the group is the one variable it isn't allowed to control.

The mechanism people reach for — Brooks' Law, adding people to a late project makes it later — is usually explained as a knowledge-transfer cost, and that explanation is what makes the mistake repeatable. Onboarding cost is primarily a **social system** problem. What a working team has built is a set of coordination patterns: who to ask, whose estimates to discount, when silence means agreement, how much half-formed thinking is safe to say out loud. Every one of those is relational, and none can be rushed or reissued to a new person on request. Adding a member perturbs all of them at once, at exactly the moment the team has least slack to re-form them, and the slack it does have is being spent on the person who caused the perturbation.

Look at what the decision assumed. It read a team as a throughput function of headcount — which is the DP1 model of work, individuals contributing fragments that a manager integrates. In that model adding a unit is obviously rational. In the actual system, capacity lives in the relationships between the parts, so adding a part subtracts capacity first and may add it later, if the team is given the authority and the time to integrate the person. Here it was given neither, and the result gets read afterwards as a bad consultant or a team that wouldn't collaborate — the two available explanations that both point at people rather than at who was allowed to decide.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — give the team genuine authority over its own membership, not a consultative role in someone else's staffing decision.**
- Move the decision, not the ritual: the team decides whether it needs someone, what the gap is, who joins, and when. Interview input with the call made elsewhere is involvement theatre applied to hiring — it produces the same resentment and none of the ownership.
- Give the team the pace as well as the choice. Absorbing a person has a real cost that lands entirely inside the team, so the team is the only unit positioned to say what rate it can sustain.
- Treat "we're behind" as a question about the work system before it is a question about headcount. Where is the variance actually being created, and does the team have the authority to control it at that point? Adding a body to a team that can't control its own dependencies just adds a body waiting on the same dependency.
- When capability genuinely is missing, hire into a whole piece of work rather than into the gaps, and expect the team to be slower for a while. That cost is real, and it should be budgeted rather than denied.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Get in front of the decision. Say early and in writing what the constraint actually is — a dependency, an unmade decision, a missing environment — so "add a person" isn't the only lever visible from above.
- If someone is being added anyway, negotiate the terms you can: a named integrator, an explicit slower two weeks, and one whole area they own rather than a queue of fragments. All three are cheaper to ask for than the outcome of not asking.
- Do not put it on the new person. They walked into a group whose boundary was drawn without them, and the awkwardness they meet is the design's, not theirs. Saying that out loud is often what makes the integration possible at all.
