# Playing politics

*Dysfunction `#69` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7492814478690992128-ewt9) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Getting a straightforward thing done depends on knowing the right person, not on following the stated process.
- Outcomes turn on who owes whom a favour and who has the ear of which senior leader; the formal route is something you file, not something that decides.
- New joiners learn within weeks that the org chart and the process documents describe a system that isn't the one running.
- Some people get very good at this and rise; others find it exhausting and demoralising, and read their own difficulty as a personal failing.
- Everyone knows it is how the place works. Nobody says so in a meeting.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Political behaviour here is a structural output, not a personality trait. Two conditions produce it, and both are design choices: formal authority is concentrated at the top, and the resources that matter — headcount, budget, platform time, a slot on the roadmap — are scarce and allocated by competition. Put those together and informal influence becomes the *only* reliable route to getting work done. The people working the network aren't being cynical; they are reading the system accurately and responding sensibly. That is the uncomfortable part: the political operator is better adapted to the structure than the person patiently following the process.

**DP1**'s deeper shape is a hierarchy of personal dominance, in which power over others is the working currency. Once that is the currency, everything scarce becomes something to be won from a person rather than resolved against the task, and relationships with the people who control allocation become the highest-return investment anyone can make. Herbst — working alongside Fred Emery — made the sharper point: a bureaucratic organisation may be unable to function or survive *without* informal practices and relationships growing up inside it. The network is not a corruption of the formal system. It is the compensating mechanism that lets a structure incapable of coordinating itself get anything done at all.

That inverts the usual remedy, and explains why anti-politics campaigns backfire. More transparency, tighter governance, a clearer RACI, a values poster about collaboration — each attacks the compensation while leaving the concentration of authority and the scarcity untouched. You remove the workaround without removing the need for one, so the work slows and the network simply goes further underground, where it is now both necessary and unspeakable. And because the behaviour genuinely does look like character, the organisation reaches for character-based fixes: hire for humility, coach the empire-builder, run an integrity module. Nothing structural changes, and the next person in that seat plays the same game.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — remove the concentration of authority worth lobbying, and there is nothing to lobby for.**
- Move coordination to the group doing the work: a multi-skilled team with a whole task, an outcome it owns, and the authority to make the calls the work requires. When decisions no longer need a patron, patronage stops paying.
- Attack the scarcity as well as the hierarchy. Standing teams with durable funding remove most of the competitive allocation that politics feeds on; annual scrambles for headcount and project money recreate it every cycle (see [[budgets-are-bureaucracy]]).
- Make allocation decisions that must stay central visible and rule-based — who decided, on what basis, against which criteria. Discretion exercised privately is what makes access to the decider valuable.
- Check what the reward systems are actually pricing. Where promotion and pay track individual visibility and territory rather than the performance of a whole task, the organisation is paying people to play (see [[pay-and-reward]], [[career-paths]]).

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Name the mechanism, not the people. "Decisions about this work sit three levels up and are allocated case by case" is a claim about design that can be discussed; "there's too much politics here" is an accusation, and it will be heard as one.
- Stop treating the informal network as illegitimate while you still depend on it. Use it honestly and in the open — say who you talked to and why — rather than pretending the formal route did the work.
- Make the cost legible. Track how long decisions actually take and how many of them needed an informal intervention to move. That turns a mood into evidence, which is the only form in which this travels upward.
- Extend some cover to the people who are bad at it. The colleague who finds this exhausting is not lacking resilience; they are refusing to spend their working life on a game the structure invented.
