# Back to the office

*Dysfunction `#78` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7496913025791102977-Wngy/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- An email announces a full-time return to the office. The reasons given are collaboration, culture, and mentoring the junior staff.
- Three years of remote work left the productivity numbers where they were. Strong teams stayed strong; the teams that struggled struggled in exactly the same way.
- The mandate makes no distinction between those two groups — it is applied to the whole population, as if the problem were the location rather than the teams.
- Meetings that used to run on video are now booked in a room, and half the room dials in anyway, because someone is travelling, sick, or has children to collect.
- Nobody can name the specific coordination that was failing, or say what will now become possible that wasn't before.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The stated reasons are testable and they do not survive the test: if the problem were collaboration, the mandate would target the teams that collaborate badly; if it were mentoring, it would change how juniors are paired with the work. A blanket policy that ignores which teams are which is not aimed at a coordination failure. It is aimed at something else, and OST names it precisely.

DP1 is not merely "control sits above the work" — Emery's fuller description is **personal dominance**: a master-servant relation in which those above hold the right to tell those below what to do and how to do it. A relation of that kind has to be *performed* to stay real. Remote work leaves the formal decision rights completely untouched; what it removes is the stage on which those rights are normally exercised in view of everyone. The walk past the desks, the visible summons, the room that fills when you call a meeting — none of that is coordination, and all of it is how dominance is seen. Take away the stage and the authority is intact but invisible, which under DP1 is close to the same thing as diminished.

This is Emery's **egocentrism**, the communication pattern DP1 produces: what has to be protected and displayed is individual position, not the whole task. It explains why the argument arrives dressed in task language — culture, collaboration, mentoring — while the policy is indifferent to the task. It also explains why the hybrid compromise satisfies nobody: half the room on video restores the room without restoring the stage. A 2026 study led by Adam Grant found leaders with more power more strongly opposed to remote work, and linked that to narcissistic traits. OST gets to the same place without needing anyone to be a narcissist: the structure rewards visible dominance, so people in it seek visible dominance. That is a design fault, not a character one.

None of which makes co-location worthless. Distance genuinely does cost a team something — see `#47` — and some work genuinely wants a room. The dysfunction is the substitution: a real, specific, local coordination need being answered with a uniform mandate that no one can trace back to any particular piece of work.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — make co-location a decision about specific work, taken by the people who do it, rather than a uniform mandate that restores visibility.**
- Force the question down to the task. Which coordination is failing, in which team, on which work? Presence follows that answer; where there is no answer, there is no need.
- Give teams control of their own working pattern as part of owning a whole task. Deciding when the work needs a room is variance control at the point of work, and it is precisely the class of decision DP2 puts with the team.
- Fund what distance actually removed instead of legislating attendance: deliberate social maintenance, real onboarding structures for juniors, and time together scheduled against the work that needs it.
- Fix the underlying need for visibility. If managers cannot tell how the work is going without seeing people at desks, the reporting relationship — not the seating plan — is the thing that is broken.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Ask, neutrally and in public, what specifically the mandate is expected to change and how we would know. It is a fair question, it is hard to answer, and asking it moves the conversation from loyalty to design.
- Bring the evidence you already have: which teams collaborate well, which don't, and the fact that the split does not track location. Data that separates the two groups is the strongest argument against a blanket rule.
- Negotiate at the team level rather than the policy level. "These two days, because that's when we do X together" concedes the room and keeps the reason attached to the work.
- Read the resistance charitably in both directions. People pushing back are not disloyal, and leaders pushing the mandate are usually not power-hungry — they are responding to a structure that made their authority invisible. Naming that turns a fight about commitment into a conversation about how coordination is meant to work.
