# Becoming Teal

*Dysfunction `#77` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491979355821330432-Qngx/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Everyone has read *Reinventing Organizations*. Job titles have become roles, approval chains have become an advice process, and the intranet has a page explaining the colours.
- Some teams genuinely thrive on it. Others are quietly confused about who decides what, and cover the gap by asking their old manager anyway.
- Middle managers resist — and are read as the obstacle — while diffusion past the early adopters stalls without anyone being able to say exactly where.
- Every setback gets the same diagnosis: *not ready yet, more inner work to do*. The remedy is always another round of development, never a different structure.
- The vocabulary is fluent and the work is unchanged. The organisation is not becoming Teal; it is becoming better at talking about Teal.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Laloux's model is an **organicist** one: organisations are held to pass through developmental stages, and Teal is the stage you reach when the collective consciousness is ready for it. That framing quietly relocates the cause of failure from the design of the work to the maturity of the people doing it — which is why the diagnosis is never falsifiable. If it works, the organisation had evolved. If it doesn't, it hadn't evolved *yet*. A framework that explains its own failures by the readiness of its adopters cannot be learned from, and an organisation running on one will keep spending on development while the structure that produces the symptoms sits untouched.

OST makes the opposite claim, and it is the more useful one: the location of control and coordination is a **design principle** — a choice, not a stage. Under DP1 it sits a level above the work; under DP2 it sits with the people doing the work. You do not grow into DP2, you decide on it, and you can decide on it on a Tuesday with a workforce exactly as enlightened as the one you have. This is also what separates the teams that thrive from the teams that are confused. Roles-instead-of-titles and an advice process are surface forms; where they happened to shift real authority over the work, the team works, and where only the vocabulary changed, people fall back to the manager who is still, structurally, accountable.

Which is why the middle managers resist, and why calling that a readiness problem misses it entirely. A half-completed Teal rollout leaves them answerable for outcomes they no longer control — the worst position the structure can put anyone in, and resisting it is the rational response, not a developmental deficit. Hjorteland's counter-example is **Buurtzorg**: its nurses did not reach a higher stage of consciousness. Jos de Blok made a structural decision about where coordination lives — small teams owning the whole task, handling variance at the point of care, with a back office that supports rather than directs. The context is not transferable and copying the practices will not reproduce the result. The principle is transferable, and it is the only part worth copying.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — stop treating DP2 as a stage to be reached and make it a design decision to be taken, named as such.**
- Say out loud which design principle each unit is running on, and where the boundary between the two now falls. "We are moving control of this class of decision from the manager to the team" is a sentence with a testable consequence; "we are becoming Teal" is not.
- Redesign whole tasks, not job titles. A team needs a slice of work it can own end to end and the authority to absorb the variance in it — otherwise the advice process is just a longer route to the same approval.
- Fix the manager role in the same move rather than leaving it to erode. Responsibility without control is the fault the rollout introduces; either the accountability moves with the authority, or the role is redesigned into one that supports the work from outside it.
- Let the people who do the work redesign it, participatively — not as a maturity assessment of whether they are ready, but as the design activity itself. Readiness is an output of doing this, never a precondition.
- Steal Buurtzorg's decision, not its org chart. Ask what de Blok actually chose about coordination, then ask what the equivalent choice is in your work.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Pick one decision your team currently escalates and get it formally, permanently moved down, with the accountability moved with it. One real transfer teaches more than a whole colour vocabulary.
- When the "not ready yet" diagnosis appears, ask the falsifying question in the room: what would we expect to see if the structure, rather than the readiness, were the problem? It is a fair question and it is very hard to answer while holding the developmental line.
- Defend the middle managers out loud. They are not blocking the change; they are holding responsibility the change took the authority away from. Naming that converts an argument about their mindset into a conversation about the design.
- Keep using the local language if it buys goodwill — but translate every proposal into who decides what, and let that translation be the thing you actually agree on.
