# Somebody has to chase it

*Dysfunction `#90` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7500084751702065152-5FCj/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The reorganisation worked, as far as it went. Stream-aligned teams own their backlogs and ship without asking a steering committee for permission. People can feel the difference.
- What didn't disappear with the old org chart is the dependencies between teams. They just stopped belonging to anyone.
- A platform change that three teams need gets raised in three different Slack channels and quietly dies in two of them. The one that gets built is the one where a person followed up — twice, then a third time.
- That person isn't the platform team's manager and can't reprioritise anyone's backlog. They are simply the one who keeps asking, and everybody has learned that if they stop asking, it stops moving.
- Nobody experiences this as a design flaw. It reads as one unusually diligent colleague, and it gets rewarded as such — right up until they move team.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Bion named this pattern **pairing**: one of the basic assumptions a group falls into when it isn't structured to work on its task — an unconscious reliance on a few individuals to generate the movement the group cannot produce on its own. It is not a personality outcome. Put any competent, conscientious person in that gap and they will fill it, because the alternative is watching necessary work not happen. Merrelyn Emery's organisational instrument measures the condition directly, as a single item: how many people report that a few must personally push things forward for anything to happen at all. When that item scores high, you are not looking at heroes; you are looking at a missing mechanism with people standing in for it.

The specific missing mechanism here is **horizontal coordination**, and Team Topologies is unusually good at hiding its absence. Done well, the topology genuinely increases *control* — teams own more of their own work and answer to fewer layers above them. That is a real DP2 gain and worth having. But control and coordination are two different things, and only one of them is on the diagram. The boxes and the interaction modes *assume* negotiation between teams; they do not design it. No forum, no standing commitment, no shared claim on capacity — nothing that makes "settle this dependency" somebody's actual job, with the authority that would let them settle it.

So the org has removed the layer that used to do cross-team arbitration without replacing what that layer did. The old structure coordinated badly, from too far above the work — but it coordinated. The new one coordinates informally, through whoever notices the gap and cares enough to close it, which is worse in one decisive respect: informal pushing has no mandate, no capacity, and no successor. It also quietly reintroduces DP1 by the back door, because the chaser ends up doing exactly what the removed manager did — holding the picture of the whole and nagging the parts — only without the standing to make a call.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — design the horizontal negotiation, don't assume the topology produces it.**
- Give cross-team dependencies an owning mechanism at the same level as the work: a standing negotiation between the teams that share the dependency, with the authority to commit capacity — not an escalation path upward and not a ticket queue.
- Make a share of each team's capacity explicitly available for work other teams need, and let the teams involved allocate it between themselves. A dependency that has to win against a team's own roadmap on that team's own terms will always lose.
- Design the platform team's intake as a two-way negotiation with its consumers, not a request box. If three teams want the same change, that fact should be visible to all four teams without anyone forwarding it.
- Treat "a few people push everything forward" as a measurable structural signal and watch it, rather than as a fact about those people. Emery's item is one question; ask it.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Make the chasing visible as work. Put the dependency, its three requesting teams and its chaser somewhere all of them can see. The point isn't credit — it's that an invisible mechanism can't be argued about, and a visible one can.
- Get the requesting teams talking to each other directly rather than each to the platform team. Three separate asks read as three low-priority requests; one shared ask reads as a priority, and it makes the missing forum's absence concrete.
- Stop being the single channel deliberately, even where it costs you something. If it only moves when you ask, that dependency is one holiday away from failing, and the failure is more useful information than your continued success at preventing it.
- Reframe it when it comes up: this is not a diligence problem or a caring problem, and the answer is not to find more people who chase. The topology bought control and left coordination undesigned — name that, and the conversation can move somewhere.
