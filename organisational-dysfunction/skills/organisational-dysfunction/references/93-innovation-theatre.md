# Innovation theatre

*Dysfunction `#93` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7501899829694963712-j3j5/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A hackathon (or innovation day, ideas jam, dragons' den) is announced to make the organisation "more innovative". Teams form around problems they pick, build prototypes over a weekend, pitch to judges, and win prizes. The energy is unmistakable.
- On Monday the winning ideas go into the normal machinery — the backlog, the innovation funnel, the next planning cycle — and quietly never come out.
- The people who built them go back to their day jobs and learn not to ask where their ideas went.
- Leadership reads the vanishing ideas as a follow-through or prioritisation problem and plans a better-run event with a clearer handover.
- Each year fewer people sign up, and the drop gets explained as fading enthusiasm.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

For the length of the event, the hackathon **is a DP2 structure**. People choose the problem, set their own goals, coordinate peer-to-peer and own a whole piece of work end to end. The energy is not a product of pizza and deadlines; it is what Emery and Trist's **six psychological requirements for productive work** feel like when they are all met at once — elbow room to decide, learning with fast feedback, variety, mutual support, meaningful whole tasks, and a future you can see yourself building. The organisation has run a live demonstration of self-managing work, and it worked.

Then it sends everyone back into DP1, where **design authority is retained above the work**, and asks the ideas to travel through it. They can't. The structure that generated them has no foothold in the structure that would have to act on them: the people with the knowledge and the motivation have no authority to build, and the people with the authority weren't in the room and have their own queue. What looks like a follow-through failure is **two incompatible design principles running side by side** — one for special events, one for everything else — and the everyday one always wins, because it owns the budget, the roadmap and people's time.

That makes the event worse than neutral. In a DP1 frame, a showcase of participation **harvests** ideas from people who have no power to act on what they've contributed. The shrinking sign-up list is not apathy; it is people drawing an accurate conclusion about how this organisation really works. The hackathon taught them that, far more clearly than any values statement could.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — stop confining DP2 to the weekend and give the teams who do the work the authority to act on what they find, every week.**
- Ask what the hackathon did that the working week doesn't: teams chose the problem, set the goal, had the skills in the room and could decide without sign-off. Move those conditions into standing teams, rather than trying to get the ideas out of the event and into the hierarchy.
- Put design authority where the knowledge is. Participative Design Workshops let teams redesign their own work so that improving it is part of the job, not a prize-winning exception to it.
- If an idea needs resources beyond a team, route it to people who can say yes within weeks and give the originating team a real part in building it — not to a funnel that exists to filter.
- Measure honestly: count ideas that reached the people they were meant for, not events held or pitches received.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Don't run another one until something downstream is different. Before the next event, secure a named decision-maker with budget and a promise of a yes/no with reasons for every winning idea within a fixed window, and publish what happened to last year's winners.
- On what you control, let your own team spend real time on the problem they cared about in the hackathon — even one idea carried through by its originators outweighs a dozen pitches.
- Name it accurately upward: the falling sign-ups aren't lost enthusiasm, they're feedback on what happens after the event. The fix isn't a better hackathon; it's a week that looks more like one.
