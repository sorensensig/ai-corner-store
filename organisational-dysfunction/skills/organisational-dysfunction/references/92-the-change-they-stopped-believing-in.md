# The change they stopped believing in

*Dysfunction `#92` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7500092686117527552-hate/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The fourth transformation in seven years is launching — agile, then DevOps, then product thinking, now a platform strategy. Each arrived with a consultant, a framework, renamed roles and a deck saying this time is different.
- Every round, people paid in full: new tools learned, new ceremonies attended, new reporting lines absorbed, working relationships rebuilt after the reorg cut them.
- Every round, the same people ended up doing the same work inside the same structure, now with a new vocabulary on top.
- The current initiative has the strongest executive sponsorship yet, and the teams are quiet — not hostile, just quiet. Surveys get filled in, workshops get attended, and everyone waits for it to pass.
- Leadership reads the silence as cynicism or change fatigue, and plans a better launch.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The people in the room are carrying two ledgers. One is **cost**: every initiative extracted real disruption — relearned workflows, replaced tooling, relationships severed by restructuring, expertise made obsolete by the next method. The other is **evidence**: across four initiatives the frameworks, the consultants and the vocabulary all changed, and the **design principle** never did. Each was a DP2 idea announced into a DP1 structure and run on DP1, so the promised improvement had no structural route to arrive. What gets labelled resistance or exhaustion is pattern recognition, and it is accurate.

The mechanism that turns this into disbelief is itself DP1. Where coordination and control sit above the work, the people doing the work have no way to decide whether a change that lands on them is worth its cost. They cannot decline it, shape it or stop it early; they receive it and absorb it. Absorb enough rounds and what accumulates is not a mood but a calculation learned from their own data: the disruption will be real and the improvement will not. No quality of sponsorship answers that calculation, because sponsorship is one of the few things that has already varied.

It also withdraws one of Emery and Trist's **six psychological requirements for productive work** — **a desirable future**. People invest in change only when they can see it leading somewhere better for them, and repeated proof that it doesn't is precisely how that requirement is taken away. The organisation then relaunches, surveys for engagement and treats the quiet as a culture problem, while the most accurate reading in the building is the one the teams have already made. The problem isn't that they are tired of change. It's that they are right.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — change the design principle this time, and give the people who bear a change's cost a real say in whether and how it happens.**
- Don't launch the next framework. Name what the last four had in common — authority over the work never moved — and make moving it the scope of the change, not a later phase.
- Put the decision where the cost lands. Participative Design Workshops let teams analyse and redesign their own work, including judging which parts of an initiative are worth the disruption to them. A change people chose to pay for is a different transaction from one they were billed for.
- Change fewer things and let them finish. Disbelief built from evidence only yields to evidence: one visible shift in who decides, held long enough to deliver, does more than another programme with a better launch.
- Retire "cynicism" as a diagnosis. Treat the teams' read of the previous rounds as data about the design, and ask them what didn't change — they already know.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Don't sell this one harder. Acknowledge the history plainly — four rounds, real cost, the same structure afterwards — before asking for anything; credibility starts with agreeing with what people have correctly observed.
- On whatever you control, let your team decide how much of the new initiative to adopt and at what pace, and protect the working relationships and expertise the last reorg left intact.
- Name it accurately upward: the quiet isn't resistance to be managed, it's a forecast based on track record — and only a different outcome changes a forecast.
