# Deskilled by AI

*Dysfunction `#84` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7496914113839669248-hOUc/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Developers who used to shape whole codebases now mostly write prompts, review diffs they did not reason their way to, and accept suggestions they could not have produced themselves.
- The assistant lands unevenly: the few people who still hold breadth get faster, while the ones confined to a narrow slice get *replaced within that slice* and have nothing else to stand on.
- Design conversations thin out. Nobody argues about the shape of the thing any more, because the shape arrives pre-formed and arguing costs more than accepting.
- Juniors are onboarded straight into the narrow job and never acquire the depth their seniors are quietly running on.
- Leadership reads the throughput numbers as proof the rollout worked, and treats the unease about judgement and craft as nostalgia.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The deskilling did not start with the tool. It started when the jobs were designed. **DP1 — redundancy of parts — requires simple, replaceable parts**, and it gets them the only way it can: by narrowing roles until each person holds a fragment small enough that losing them costs little. Ten years of that produces exactly the workforce the AI rollout then meets. The assistant does not arrive at a developer who designs systems; it arrives at someone already reduced to the slice of work the tool happens to handle best. What looks like AI taking the skill away is AI *completing* a process the organisation began long before anyone had heard of a coding assistant.

This is why the same tool produces opposite outcomes in two organisations, and why the tools debate is nearly always the wrong debate. Under **DP2 — redundancy of functions**, where a multiskilled group owns a whole task and holds control over its own methods, the assistant extends reach: the group still holds the design intent, still controls variance at the point where it arises, and now disposes of more capacity to act on it. Under DP1 the identical tool substitutes for depth that the structure never permitted to develop. The technology is the same. The **job design is upstream of it**, and it decides which of the two you get.

Herbst's point sits underneath this: work produces people as surely as it produces goods and services. A structure that spent a decade manufacturing narrow, replaceable specialists has produced people who cannot absorb this tool, and it has done so on purpose — not out of malice, but because redundancy of parts cannot be run any other way. Read against the six psychological job requirements, the loss is precise: **continuous learning** and **variety** are the first casualties of a narrow role, and once they are gone, the elbow room to use a powerful new tool well is gone with them. So the honest question before any rollout is not whether the tool is good. It is what kind of person the organisation has spent the last decade producing — because that, not the model, determines what the tool will do here.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — the tool amplifies whatever job design it lands in, so redesign the jobs before arguing about the tool.**
- Make the unit of ownership a **whole task held by a multiskilled group**, not a fragment held by an individual. Breadth is the precondition for using an assistant as an extension rather than a substitute.
- Restore control over method to the people doing the work: which parts to delegate to the tool, which to keep by hand, and when to overrule it are decisions that belong at the point of work, not in a rollout mandate from above.
- Design **continuous learning and variety back into the roles** explicitly — rotation across the whole task, time where the hard part is done unassisted, real design authority. A skill that is never exercised does not survive the tool.
- Judge the rollout on whether people's capability grew, not on throughput. Rising output alongside shrinking depth is the failure mode, and it will read as success for a year or two.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Keep doing the hard parts by hand often enough to stay able to. Use the assistant where you could already have done the work yourself; be deliberate where you couldn't.
- In your own team, widen the slice: pair across boundaries, rotate who owns which area, make sure juniors get whole problems and not just prompt-and-review.
- Reframe the conversation when the unease gets dismissed as resistance. The concern is not that the tool is bad — it is that narrow roles leave no depth for it to extend. That lands with leadership in a way "we don't like AI" never will.
- Be careful with the individual reading. "People need to upskill" relocates a design consequence into the person carrying it, and asks them to fix on their own time what the structure produced on the organisation's.
