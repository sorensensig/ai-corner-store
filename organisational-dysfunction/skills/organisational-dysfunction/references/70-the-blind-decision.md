# The blind decision

*Dysfunction `#70` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491966478808707072-PYtq) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A senior leader formally approves a major technical or systems choice, then cannot say afterwards what was actually decided or why one option beat the others.
- The people who understood the trade-offs — domain experts, the cross-functional team, the specialists — did the real evaluation, wrote the comparison across every relevant dimension, and reached a conclusion weeks before the approval meeting.
- The approval meeting is short. It is a summary, a nod, and a signature; nobody expects the decider to interrogate the analysis, and nobody would have time to bring them up to speed if they did.
- The brief gets written for the approver's calendar rather than for the decision: compressed into a slide, stripped of the uncertainty that mattered most.
- When the choice later turns out badly, accountability lands on the signature — which was the one part of the process that carried no judgement.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This is **DP1** in its purest form: formal authority over decisions rises with seniority while the knowledge that decisions depend on stays down where the work is. The two are in different places, and the hierarchy is what keeps them there. Herbst — working alongside Fred Emery — put the mechanism plainly: the higher the level, the more purely a person becomes a decision-maker without tasks to perform; the lower the level, the more purely an executor without decisions to make. Follow that gradient far enough and you arrive here, at a decision-maker with nothing left to decide with.

Notice what the ritual is actually doing. The CTO is not being lazy or negligent; they are being asked to supply something the structure has already consumed. The organisation has done the thinking, and the sign-off exists to convert that thinking into something the hierarchy recognises as authoritative. So the approver signs for an organisation that has already decided. The signature transfers no information and adds no judgement — it launders a distributed technical conclusion into a single accountable name.

The cost is not the wasted hour. It is that variance now gets controlled in the wrong place. The people closest to the work can see the assumptions that are shakiest and the conditions under which the choice would need revisiting, but they hold no authority to act on that later; the person who holds the authority never absorbed the assumptions and so cannot recognise when they break. The decision becomes unrevisable in practice, because revisiting it would mean reopening a question nobody at the authority level ever really held. And the approver's own role is hollowed out in the process — a job of consequence with no task in it, which is exactly the arrangement Emery and Trist's six psychological job requirements predict people will find corrosive, seniority notwithstanding.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — put the decision where the knowledge already is, instead of shipping the knowledge upward to meet the authority.**
- Delegate the decision *class*, not the individual decision: name which choices the team makes and closes, and stop routing those through a signature. A team with a whole task needs the authority the task requires, including the irreversible-looking parts of it.
- Reserve senior involvement for the questions seniority can actually answer — the constraints, the risk appetite, the commitments already made elsewhere in the organisation — and put those in *before* the evaluation, where they change the analysis, rather than after it, where they can only rubber-stamp it.
- If a call genuinely needs to sit above the team because it binds several parts of the business, then get the deciders into the work itself rather than into a summary of it: joint evaluation with the experts, not a brief delivered to a calendar slot.
- Make revisiting the decision somebody's actual authority. Record the assumptions the choice rests on and give the team that holds them the standing to reopen it when they break.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Write the brief for the decision rather than for the approval. Lead with the two or three places the recommendation could be wrong and what would have to be true for the alternative to win — that is the only part a decider can meaningfully engage with in the time available.
- State the recommendation as the team's decision and ask explicitly for the constraints you may not know about, rather than asking for a verdict. It is a smaller ask, and it is the one that actually improves the outcome.
- Record the assumptions and the review trigger in writing at the point of decision, whoever signs. It is the cheapest way to keep a choice revisable in a structure that would otherwise freeze it.
- Don't read the empty sign-off as the approver's failing. They are occupying a position the design created, and they are as constrained by it as you are.
