# It took an outsider to say it

*Dysfunction `#87` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7496917891867459584-9YVs/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Someone inside has been naming the same problem for a year or two — in retros, in one-to-ones, in Slack, in a document with the data attached — and it keeps landing just below the cut line.
- A consultant is brought in, spends a few weeks, and presents the same finding in a deck. Leadership is visibly engaged; something moves within the month.
- The consultant's version is not better. Often it is thinner, and drawn largely from interviews with the people who had already said it.
- Nobody registers the irony. The internal advocate is not vindicated, not consulted on the fix, and frequently not even in the room when it is decided.
- That person goes quiet afterwards — not sulking, but having correctly worked out that content was never the variable.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

A DP1 structure does not just concentrate authority above the work, it makes communication **asymmetrical**. Messages travel up through the hierarchy and are weighted on the way by the sender's position as much as by their content. A signal from below is not evaluated on its merits and rejected; it is discounted before it is evaluated, because the channel prices the sender. This is a property of the design, not a judgement about anyone's competence, and it is why raising the same point louder, or with better data, or through one more channel, does not work. Every one of those routes runs through the same filter.

The consultant bypasses the filter by standing outside the structure entirely. They hold no position in it, so their message carries none of the accumulated weight of power relations — no history of having been overruled, no standing that a manager's own standing is measured against, nothing owed and nothing at stake. What looks like fresh insight is mostly an unfiltered channel. Gerald Weinberg's old observation applies exactly: organisations hire consultants so that someone will say what their own people have been saying, in a voice the structure is able to receive.

Read that way, the episode is not a story about a good consultant. It is a **measurement of the organisation's own information channel** — and the reading is bad. The people closest to the work detect variance first and most accurately; a structure that systematically discounts them is discarding its best early-warning system and then paying an outside firm to reconstruct it at a lag of eighteen months. The organisation did not need someone who was right. It needed someone who would be heard, and that requirement is the diagnosis.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — stop routing judgement upward for validation, and the sender's position stops being the deciding variable.**
- Put the decision where the knowledge already is. If the people who can see the problem can also act on it, nothing has to survive a trip through the filter to become real.
- When you do commission outside work, name the internal people who hold the same view and make them co-authors of the finding, not interview subjects quoted anonymously in an appendix.
- Treat "we needed an outsider to say it" as an incident worth reviewing on its own terms: who said it first, when, through which channel, and what stopped it. That review is more valuable than the consultant's recommendations.
- Build peer-to-peer routes for variance that do not climb the hierarchy — people doing adjacent work talking directly, with the authority to change something between them.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Recognise the filter for what it is rather than concluding you argued it badly. The failure is positional; two more years of better framing will not move it.
- Borrow altitude deliberately where you can — get the point carried by someone the structure already weights heavily, and be explicit that you are doing so rather than hoping to be noticed.
- If a consultant is engaged in your area, get into the engagement early. Being a named source shifts you from discounted to cited, which is a real if unsatisfying change in weight.
- Say the quiet part once, calmly, when the finding lands: that it was raised internally on these dates. Done without grievance it is data about the channel, and it is occasionally the thing that gets heard.
