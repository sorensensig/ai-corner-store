# The facilitator's toolkit

*Dysfunction `#65` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7490648023962808321-W1mF) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The workshop is textbook-clean: icebreaker, post-its, rotation stations, dot voting, an energiser when the room dips. Nothing goes wrong.
- People are present but not quite *in* it — polite, cooperative, going through the motions of participation.
- The outputs are adequate. Nobody complains. Nobody is gripped either.
- Afterwards it gets read as a facilitation problem: the wrong exercise, not enough warm-up, a room that "wasn't ready" — so next time the toolkit gets bigger.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Every tool in the kit carries an implicit answer to the only question that really matters in the room: **who owns this?** The facilitator introduces the activity, calls time, runs the energiser, and closes the vote. However collaborative the exercises *feel*, the sequence says plainly that someone above the group is coordinating the group's work. That is DP1 — and it is exactly what a participative workshop is supposed not to be.

Merrelyn Emery's point about the **first educational paradigm** is what bites here: most of the standard facilitation repertoire was built inside DP1, where an expert holds the design and the participants supply content into slots the expert has already shaped. Dropping those tools into a session that is trying to run on DP2 doesn't produce a useful hybrid — it produces confusion about who actually has control. The room gets two contradictory signals and has to pick one, and it picks the one the structure is enforcing.

What follows isn't apathy. Dependency (wait to be told what the next activity is) and flight (drift, comply, disengage) are **Bion's basic assumptions** doing their job: they are accurate readings of the design principle actually operating in the room. The participants were willing. The toolkit told them they weren't in charge, and they believed it.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — hand the group authority over the session itself, not just over the sticky notes inside it.**
- Use a format built on DP2 from the ground up rather than retrofitted onto DP1 exercises: the Participative Design Workshop and Search Conference tradition, where participants analyse their own situation, reach their own conclusions, and own the design of the work that follows.
- Move the facilitator's role from *running the content* to *guarding the structure* — hold the conditions and the time, and stop making the substantive calls (which item wins, when the conversation is finished, what the group's output means).
- Kill the mechanisms that quietly reserve the decision for the front of the room. Dot voting is the clearest one: it converts a group's reasoning into a tally that the facilitator then closes on the group's behalf.

**If you can't change the whole format yet:**
- Audit your own kit tool by tool with one question — *who owns the outcome of this exercise?* Drop or rewrite the ones where the honest answer is "me". That is usually a shorter list than it feels like.
- Give the group the agenda early enough to change it. Being able to alter the plan is a far stronger ownership signal than any amount of interactive exercise inside a fixed plan.
- When engagement sags, resist the reflex to reach for a better activity. Name the ambiguity instead ("who decides what we do with this?") — the sag is information about the structure, not a cue for another energiser.
