# The wellness programme

*Dysfunction `#83` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7500062697670082560-D0EK/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The organisation launches a mental-health initiative: an employee assistance programme, a subscription to a wellness app, mindfulness at lunch, posters about checking in on each other.
- Leadership starts talking about work–life balance at all-hands, and a new policy discourages email after six.
- HR reports the uptake numbers and they look good — sign-ups are strong, sessions are attended, the initiative is judged a success on its own terms.
- Stress-related sick leave does not move. Not down, not up. The same people burn out on the same schedule.
- Eventually someone asks, carefully, whether any of it is doing anything — and the answer is another round of the same thing, better promoted.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The programme is aimed at the strain the work produces, not at the work. That distinction is the whole diagnosis. De Guerre, Emery, Aughton and Trull, working across Canadian and Australian organisational data, found that **organisational structure is the single largest factor in employee mental health** — ahead of individual coping skills, ahead of access to wellness resources, ahead of awareness. And the causal path they traced runs one way only: the **design principle** determines the enabling conditions — autonomy, trust, the quality of relationships — and those conditions determine mental health and sick days. Structure is upstream of everything the wellness programme touches.

Which is why the gains never hold. Interventions that specialise in improving relationships, building trust, or raising awareness of mental illness can move the needle briefly, but an enabling condition cannot be held in place against the structure underneath it. Under DP1, coordination and control sit above the work: elbow room is granted rather than owned, the ability to influence what happens next is thin, and the person carrying the variance has no authority to remove its cause. A mindfulness session does not change any of that; it teaches people to tolerate it more quietly. Run the programme, and the structure keeps producing strain at exactly the rate it did before, because producing strain is not a defect in a DP1 design — it is a property of one.

Read against the six psychological job requirements, the point sharpens: elbow room, continuous learning, variety, mutual support and respect, meaningfulness, and a desirable future are all **properties of how the job is designed**, not benefits that can be procured alongside it. No app supplies them. Herbst's formulation is the uncomfortable one to sit with — work produces people as surely as it produces goods and services, and what an organisation does to the people who pass through it is an output of the design, not an unfortunate side effect of it. A wellness programme bolted onto a DP1 structure treats the exhaust as though it were the engine: the smoke is real, measuring it is honest work, and none of it reaches the thing doing the burning.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — mental health is an output of the design principle, so it moves when the design principle moves and not before.**
- Treat sick leave, turnover and burnout as **design feedback**, not as an HR caseload. If the numbers do not respond to a wellness intervention, that is information about where the cause lives.
- Move control over the work to the people doing it: let the team set its own methods, absorb its own variances, and hold the authority to change what is producing the strain. That is the intervention the evidence supports.
- Audit the actual jobs against the six requirements — where is elbow room missing, where is learning absent, where is the future undesirable — and redesign the ones that fail. This is org design work, not wellbeing work, and it belongs with whoever owns the structure.
- Keep the EAP. Genuine acute support matters and people need somewhere to go. Just stop counting its uptake as evidence that the organisation is getting healthier.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Refuse the framing where a flat sick-leave curve becomes an engagement problem. The curve is the most useful signal anyone has; let it say what it says.
- Ask what changed in the work. If the answer is "nothing", you have named the reason the programme did not land, without accusing anyone of not caring.
- Protect what autonomy exists in your own team — who decides how the work gets done, who is allowed to stop and fix a broken handoff. Local elbow room is small, real, and more protective than any app.
- Be careful with the individual reading. "People aren't resilient enough" and "people aren't using the resources" are both ways of relocating a structural cause into the person carrying it, which is its own harm on top of the original one.
