# Endless alignment meetings

*Dysfunction `#91` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7500087017670877184-1Lwy/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The calendar is full of alignment: team-to-team syncs, cross-functional check-ins, a steering group update, the all-hands, the occasional offsite to get everyone back on the same page.
- Every one of them is defensible on its own. The dependency is real, the decision genuinely involves four parties, someone genuinely does need to know.
- The volume is not defensible, and nobody owns the volume. Each meeting was added by a different person solving a different local problem.
- Meetings reliably produce more meetings — a sync surfaces something that needs a follow-up, which needs the right people, which needs a session to prepare for it.
- The organisation is spending a serious fraction of its working hours simply staying coordinated, and the people in the rooms are the ones who would otherwise be doing the work being coordinated.
- Attempts to fix it are meeting hygiene: agendas, timeboxes, no-meeting Wednesdays, a cull that lasts six weeks before the calendar refills.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

In OST terms, **high coordination cost is a reliable indicator of poorly drawn boundaries**. Where you cut the organisation determines how much talking it has to do: every dependency that crosses a boundary has to be managed by communication, because it can't be managed by the people doing the work. A structure sliced by function, technology layer, or org-chart convenience leaves each unit holding a fragment of a task, and fragments have to be continuously reassembled by conversation. The meetings are not overhead bolted onto a working structure — they *are* the structure's integration mechanism, and it is being run manually, by humans, in real time, forever.

This is why meeting hygiene never holds. Cancel a sync and the dependency it was managing does not go away; it resurfaces as a delay, a surprise, or a rework, and within a couple of months somebody rationally puts the meeting back. The calendar is a fairly accurate readout of how badly the boundaries fit the work. A DP2 structure attacks the cause rather than the symptom: a group owns a **whole task**, including responsibility for its own coordination and control, which is precisely what removes the need for external alignment work. The coordination still happens — continuously, at the point of work, between people who share the outcome — it just stops needing a room and an invitation.

There is a counterintuitive finding worth carrying here, from research in the software industry: **lack of coordination contributes more powerfully to low motivation than lack of control does.** That cuts against the reflex that autonomy is the main engagement lever. Working together toward a shared goal appears to matter more than being left alone to decide. It also explains why cutting the meetings without redrawing the boundaries makes things worse rather than better — you have removed the only place where people experienced their work as connected to anyone else's, and left the fragmentation intact. People do not resent coordinating. They resent coordinating something that should never have been split.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — redraw the boundaries so coordination is built into the work instead of scheduled around it.**
- Read the calendar as a diagnostic before touching it. Map the recurring alignment meetings to the dependencies they exist to manage; the clusters show you exactly where the boundary is cutting through a whole task.
- Reunite the fragments: give one group the whole task the meetings are currently stitching together, including responsibility for its own coordination and control. Judge the redesign by whether the meetings become unnecessary, not by whether they get cancelled.
- Where a dependency genuinely must cross a boundary, design a standing mechanism for it with the authority to settle things — not a recurring status update between people who then both have to go and ask permission.
- Fix the boundaries first, then let the calendar empty on its own. A meeting cull applied to an unchanged structure is a tax rise on the people who still have to make the dependency work.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Keep a log for a month of what each recurring meeting is actually resolving. It converts "we have too many meetings" — which everyone agrees with and nobody can act on — into a specific list of boundaries that don't fit, which is an argument that can go somewhere.
- Push decisions down to the smallest set of people who share the outcome, and let them settle it between themselves rather than reporting it into a forum. Every decision that stops needing an audience removes a meeting permanently.
- Be careful what you cancel. Cut the reporting-upward sessions before the peer-to-peer ones; the second kind is the only coordination some people have, and losing it costs more than it saves.
- Reframe it for whoever is running the efficiency drive: the meetings are filling a structural gap, so the goal is not fewer meetings but a structure that doesn't need them. Otherwise you will do this again next year.
