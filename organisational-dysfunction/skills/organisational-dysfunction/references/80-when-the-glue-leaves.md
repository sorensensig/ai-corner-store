# When the glue leaves

*Dysfunction `#80` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7494489815082668032-_yxz/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- One person quietly holds the team together: they onboard newcomers, notice the conflict before it becomes one, keep the runbook current, and know who to ask about anything.
- None of that appears in their job description, their objectives, or anyone's plan — it is visible only as an absence of problems.
- They resign, or a reorg moves them, and within weeks things that used to happen by themselves start falling between people.
- The organisation reads the gap as a management gap and hires a coordinator or team lead — someone with the coordination *skills* but none of the domain knowledge or relationships that made the work work.
- Six months on the team has both a new layer above it and less coordination than before, and nobody can quite say when the trade happened.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The glue person is not an anomaly the team got lucky with. They are the visible form of a **structural debt**: in a DP1 organisation, coordination is not a property the work group holds, so someone has to be it. The team's real coordination needs did not disappear because the org chart declined to acknowledge them — they were absorbed by a volunteer, unpaid, unnamed and therefore unmanaged. The structure's apparent smoothness was borrowed against one person's discretionary effort, and the resignation is simply the loan being called.

What happens next is Parkinson's observation about administrative structures, running exactly on schedule: confronted with a coordination gap, a bureaucracy's reflex is to **add a layer above the work** rather than build capacity inside it. This looks like the obvious remedy — coordination was missing, so appoint a coordinator — but it mistakes the *function* for the *position*. The glue person coordinated because they were embedded: they had the domain knowledge to know which variances mattered and the relationships to resolve them where they arose. A manager appointed from outside has the title and neither of the two things that made the function work. The result is a double loss — the internal coordination is gone *and* the team now carries hierarchical overhead, with each decision that used to be settled sideways in a corridor now travelling up and back down.

Underneath both moves is the same design principle. **DP1 — redundancy of parts** — makes people interchangeable fragments and puts coordination one level up; its only answer to "who coordinates?" is "an individual, and if that individual leaves, another individual". That is why key-person risk is not a staffing accident here, it is the design working as intended. **DP2 — redundancy of functions** — puts coordination *inside* the work group, distributed by design across multi-skilled members, so no single person carries it and no single departure removes it. A DP2 team does not have a glue person, because it does not have an unowned gap for one to fill. The right question is never "how do we replace them?" but "why did this whole function depend on one person in the first place?"

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — make coordination a shared function of the team, not a job one person is quietly doing.**
- Name the invisible work first: onboarding, context-holding, conflict-catching, keeping the shared knowledge current. Written down, it stops being a personality and becomes a set of functions that can be designed for.
- Distribute those functions across the team deliberately — rotate them, pair on them, hold them as group responsibilities with the authority that goes with them. Redundancy of functions is what makes a departure survivable.
- Give the team enough multi-skilling that more than one person can handle each variance where it arises. Overlap is not slack; it is the mechanism.
- Resist the reflex to appoint a coordinator above the group. If the gap is coordination *within* the work, a layer above it can only convert peer-to-peer settlement into escalation.
- If someone must be hired, hire into the work — another team member who does the task and shares the coordination — not onto a rung above it.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Make the glue visible while the glue is still there. A blunt list of "things that only work because X does them" is the cheapest possible intervention and is available to anyone on the team.
- Start spreading a single function now — let someone else run onboarding, or hand the runbook to a second owner. One deliberate transfer proves the point better than a proposal about it.
- If you *are* the glue: this is worth saying out loud rather than absorbing. The effort is real work being done off the books, and its invisibility is the structure's problem, not evidence that you should carry more of it.
- When the departure has already happened, argue the choice explicitly — a layer above versus capacity within — before the requisition is written. That framing is sayable at most altitudes, and it is much harder to unwind once the role exists.
