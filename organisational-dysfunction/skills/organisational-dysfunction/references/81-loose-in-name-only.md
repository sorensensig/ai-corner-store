# Loose in name only

*Dysfunction `#81` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7498608665231552513-Paxj/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The operating model is described as "tight–loose–tight": leadership sets the *what*, teams own the *how*, leadership checks the *result*. Everyone nods; it sounds like the reasonable middle.
- In practice the loose middle is a corridor, not a field. Teams find the walls one at a time — a tool that turns out not to be allowed, a sequencing choice that gets reversed, a scope call that needed a sign-off nobody mentioned.
- Nobody can produce the list of what is actually inside the loose part. The boundaries exist, they were simply never written down or negotiated with the people inside them.
- Autonomy is granted over method only. The objective arrives from above, and so does the judgement of whether it was met.
- Teams stop testing the edges. They deliver competently against goals they did not set, and the org reads the resulting flatness as a maturity or motivation problem.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This is one of the most sophisticated defences DP1 has produced, because it concedes exactly the thing that costs nothing. Choosing the method while someone else owns the objective and grades the outcome is not self-management — it is **delegated execution** with a better vocabulary. The hierarchy keeps the two decisions that actually constitute control (what counts as the goal, and what counts as success) and hands down the one that feels like freedom from the inside. That is why the model survives contact with reality so well: it can absorb every complaint about autonomy without ever moving authority.

The clue is in the history of the idea it borrows. Fred Emery was willing to endorse management by objectives, but only *inside* a DP2 structure — among equals who negotiate goals with each other, where setting the objective and doing the work sit in the same place. Lifted out of that context and dropped into a hierarchy, MBO inverts: the same words now describe a superior issuing targets to a subordinate. Tight–loose–tight is that inversion made into an operating model. DP1 borrows DP2's language — ownership, empowerment, autonomy — precisely because the language is popular and the structure underneath it is unchanged.

The cost is visible in variance control and in Emery's psychological job requirements. A team can only absorb the variances it has authority over; here it can adjust method but not goal, so every disturbance that implicates the objective — a wrong target, a changed environment, an assumption that has expired — must travel up, and usually doesn't, because raising it reads as failing to deliver. And the requirement Emery called the opportunity to learn on the job depends on **setting your own goals and getting feedback against them**. Split those two apart — goals from above, feedback from above — and there is nothing left to learn from except compliance. The unwritten boundary is not sloppiness, either. An unnegotiated boundary can be moved at any time without anyone having broken a promise, which is exactly what makes it useful to the level that holds it.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — a team that cannot negotiate its own goals does not have the loose part, whatever the model is called.**
- Move goal-setting into a negotiation between the team and the level above it, rather than a handover. Equals negotiating objectives is the condition MBO was designed for; without it the rest is decoration.
- Give the team authority over what counts as success — the measures, not just the method. Judgement held above the work is the same control the model claims to have released.
- Write the boundaries down and make them jointly owned: budget, risk appetite, the decisions that genuinely need to go up. A named constraint can be argued with; an unwritten one can only be discovered by hitting it.
- Match the authority to the variances the team actually meets. If the objective can go wrong in the team's hands, the team needs standing to change it.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Ask for the loose part to be made explicit — "what is inside our discretion, and what isn't?" It is a mild, reasonable question, and it converts a corridor into something the team can at least see.
- Negotiate the measure of success on the next piece of work, even if you cannot negotiate the goal. It is the smaller half of the same authority and it is often available.
- Log the reversals: every time a decision inside the "loose" middle got overturned, and by whom. The pattern is far more persuasive than the general complaint, and it is the evidence a redesign conversation needs.
- Drop the self-blame. Flat, careful delivery against goals you did not set is the predictable output of the design, not evidence that the team lacks initiative.
