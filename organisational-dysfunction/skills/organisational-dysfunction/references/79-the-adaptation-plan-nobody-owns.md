# The adaptation plan nobody owns

*Dysfunction `#79` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7494360860350545920-9AxH) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The organisation has real sustainability credentials — a certification, published green-coding standards, a reuse rate it can point to — and they were earned, not invented.
- Meanwhile AI coding assistants have been rolled out to every developer team, and nobody is tracking the compute they consume or what that costs the shared environment.
- The standards predate the technology and say nothing about it, so the gap isn't a violation of policy — there is no policy to violate.
- Asked who owns understanding the consequences, the honest answer is nobody: sustainability owns the certification, engineering owns the rollout, and the space between them is unassigned rather than contested.
- What the organisation produces on this subject is reporting — disclosures, dashboards, a figure in the annual review — and the reporting is accurate.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The environment here is what Emery and Trist called a **Type IV, turbulent field**: the ground itself is in motion, generating change that no single organisation produced and none can manage alone. Climate is the clearest case of it. The company's structure, though, is DP1 — built for **Type III**, a *disturbed-reactive* world stable enough to plan inside, where you scan the environment, form a plan, and execute against it. A plan is the right instrument for Type III. In Type IV the ground moves while you write, and the instrument fails not through bad execution but through category error.

Emery named the three ways organisations *maladapt* when they meet turbulence with a structure built for the level below, and this case shows all three at once. **Superficiality**: the certification and the reuse rate satisfy an existing, well-understood category of disclosure, and leave untouched the structure that generates new demand. **Segmentation**: the standards manage the field as it looked when they were written, so a technology that arrived afterwards falls outside their frame entirely. **Dissociation**: the rollout acts on the immediate, local productivity gain and severs the connection to consequences that land in the shared field. None of these is a failure of intent. Each is what a DP1 structure does with a Type IV problem, because DP1 can only distribute a problem into the parts it already has.

That is why the ownership gap is not fixable by assignment. Adaptation to a turbulent field is not a task that decomposes — it cannot be handed to a sustainability function, a green-coding working group, or an AI governance board, because none of them holds enough of the whole to act on it. So the request travels upward looking for a level wide enough to own it, and arrives at a level too far from the work to know anything. What comes back down is a report. **DP1 produces reports about adaptation, not adaptation itself.**

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — a turbulent field is met by shared values held across the field, not by a plan owned inside one organisation.**
- Stop searching for the owner. The absence of one is the diagnosis, not the problem: no single role can hold a whole-system consequence in a structure that only has parts.
- Put the consequence back where the work is. The teams running the assistants are the only place the compute variance actually arises, and therefore the only place it can be controlled at the point it occurs.
- Give those teams the whole task rather than a fragment of it: the tooling decision, its environmental cost, and the authority to trade one against the other. Split across three functions, it is nobody's to weigh.
- Work the problem at field level, with peers, suppliers and the wider sector — jointly-set directions and shared ideals, rather than a private plan each organisation writes and reports against.
- Treat the standards as a living instrument with the people doing the work inside the loop that revises them, so a technology arriving next year does not fall outside the frame again by default.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Measure the thing nobody measures. A rough, honest figure for what the assistants consume changes the conversation more than another governance proposal, and any team can produce one.
- Raise it as a question about *who could possibly know*, not as an accusation of neglect. The gap is structural, and framing it as negligence guarantees the answer is a new owner on an org chart.
- Name the category error where it lands well: this is not a compliance gap, it is an adaptation problem being handled with a planning instrument. That distinction is the whole argument, and it is sayable at most altitudes.
- Get the question into forums that already cross the boundary — industry groups, supplier reviews, sector working bodies — since a field-level problem needs field-level company, and those rooms already exist.
