# The bureaucracy that became the work

*Dysfunction `#68` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491025692650876928-gPML) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- You were hired to design systems. Most of your week goes on administering the administration: logging hours against project codes, preparing status updates, sitting in governance meetings.
- Approvals are sought for things that have already been decided, and documentation is produced for the record rather than for anyone who will read it.
- Every meeting has to be justified by a deliverable, and every decision has to be documented — so producing the justification becomes a deliverable in its own right.
- The administrative apparatus grows through reorganisations, cost programmes and headcount freezes alike; it is the one part of the organisation that never shrinks.
- The actual work still gets done — but around the system, in the gaps, by people quietly protecting a few uninterrupted hours.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Herbst's inversion names it exactly: the structure stops serving the task, and the task starts serving the structure. This is not laziness in the back office or a governance process that was designed badly. It is the predictable end state of **DP1** under load. When coordination and control sit above the work, every unit of real variance at the point of work has to be made legible to the level that is allowed to respond to it — logged, reported, justified, approved. That translation work is not overhead bolted onto the system; it *is* the system's control mechanism, and it scales with the amount of work being controlled.

The mechanism that makes it self-sustaining is internal turbulence. As DP1's coordination costs rise, the organisation spends more of itself on holding its own structure together, until the actual work slips into the background and becomes a *boundary condition* for maintaining the apparatus — something that has to keep happening at some minimum level so that the reporting, governing and approving can continue. Parkinson described the observable surface of this in 1955: administrative work expands to fill the time available, officials make work for one another, and the apparatus grows at a rate essentially unrelated to the work being administered. OST supplies the cause underneath the observation — officials make work for each other because a structure built on redundancy of parts can only integrate fragments by adding more integrators.

This is why efficiency drives never touch it. Trimming forms, merging two governance forums or moving the timesheet to a better tool reduces the cost of the control loop without removing the need for one, so the apparatus regrows in the shape of the new tooling. The system is doing exactly what it was designed to do. That is the finding, not the failure.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — remove the need for the control loop rather than making it cheaper to run.**
- Move variance control to the point of work: give a multi-skilled group a whole task, an outcome they own, and the authority to respond to what goes wrong without seeking permission. Most reporting exists because someone above must decide; when they no longer must, the report has no consumer.
- Audit the apparatus by consumer, not by cost. For each recurring report, forum and approval gate, name the decision it feeds and who cannot act without it. Anything whose honest answer is "for the record" or "so leadership has visibility" is a symptom of the design principle, not a governance need.
- Apply minimum critical specification: specify the outcome and the non-negotiable constraints (safety, legal, money at risk), and leave the method to the people doing the work. Every additional specification creates a new thing that must be checked.
- Fund and staff around durable teams and capabilities rather than projects. Project codes, per-project justification and per-project approval are a large share of the administrative load, and they exist because money is allocated to temporary containers (see [[budgets-are-bureaucracy]]).

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Get the load measured rather than felt. A team logging where its hours actually go for a few weeks turns "we're drowning in admin" from a complaint into evidence — and evidence is the only form in which this travels upward.
- Stop volunteering new reporting. Bureaucracy grows mostly by well-meant addition: a tracker offered to reassure a nervous stakeholder becomes permanent within a quarter.
- Kill by trial, not by argument. Pick one report or gate, agree to suspend it for six weeks, and see who notices. Suspension is far easier to get agreed than abolition, and the silence is usually the whole case.
- Be careful how you name it. "Our processes are inefficient" invites a process improvement initiative — more apparatus (see [[fixing-the-process]]). "Decisions about this work are made two levels above the work" points at the actual cause.
