# Divided for efficiency

*Dysfunction `#75` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491969524183904258-XuKj) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Twenty people arrive for a workshop and are immediately split into four groups of five, each given a different question, because "we'll cover more ground that way".
- Ninety minutes later each group presents a tidy summary, everyone nods, and the day is declared productive.
- A week on, people can recall what happened at *their* table and almost nothing from the other three — not the conclusions, and certainly not why.
- The decisions that came out of the other groups get treated as somebody else's, and quietly re-litigated later by whoever wasn't in the room for them.
- The facilitator's deck reads as one coherent outcome; the twenty people in the room hold four disconnected fragments and no shared picture.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Splitting the room is a **suboptimisation** move, and a textbook one: it optimises the parts — four questions covered in the time one would take, small groups that talk more freely — while degrading the thing the workshop actually exists to produce, which is a shared understanding across all twenty people. The parts each perform beautifully. The whole performs worse than if the room had done less, together.

The hidden cost sits in the handover. A summary transmits a conclusion; it cannot transmit the reasoning that produced it, and it never mentions the alternatives the group considered and set aside. Those discarded options are most of what a group knows by the end — the sense of where the difficulty really lay, which arguments nearly won, what the conclusion depends on. Present a summary and you hand over the residue of a conversation while keeping the conversation. **The integration cost is the whole discussion the other fifteen people never had**, and it is paid later, in re-openings and misalignment, by people who cannot say what they are missing.

Note the design principle at work. Fragmenting a task among parts and having the pieces re-integrated somewhere else — a plenary, a facilitator's synthesis, a deck — is **DP1** applied to a conversation. Each group does a piece; nobody owns the whole; the integrator holds a picture no participant has. This is why the fix is not better summaries or a longer report-back slot: those improve the transmission of fragments and leave the fragmentation itself untouched. In the **Search Conference** tradition the ordering is reversed — the room works *as a whole* on the tasks that matter, and subgroups are used sparingly and deliberately, where a task genuinely needs a smaller setting or where breaking the room up surfaces a power imbalance that plenary would mask. Integration is not the wrap-up. It is the main event, and the return to the whole is the most important part of the process.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — do fewer things with the whole room rather than more things in parallel, and design the return to the whole as the centre of the session, not its closing slot.**
- Work the questions that matter with all twenty people. Accept the reduced coverage: one question genuinely shared beats four questions held by five people each.
- If you must use subgroups, use them for a reason you can name — a task that needs a smaller setting, or a deliberate split that lets quieter voices form a position before the powerful ones fill the room. "Speed" is not one of those reasons.
- Budget integration as the largest block on the agenda, not the last fifteen minutes. If the return to the whole is where the understanding is built, it cannot be the part that gets squeezed.
- Have groups bring back their *reasoning and rejected alternatives*, not a conclusion — and let the whole room work them, with authority to change them. A report-back the room cannot alter is a presentation, and presentations do not integrate.

**If you can't change the format yet:**
- Argue for cutting the agenda rather than parallelising it. Two questions the whole room owns will move further than four that nobody does — and this is an easier sell as a scoping decision than as a facilitation critique.
- Keep every group's raw working visible on the walls for the whole session, so the material at least stays in a shared field instead of collapsing into four summaries.
- When a group reports back, ask what they *nearly* concluded and what they dropped. It is one question, it costs a minute, and it restores the part of the thinking a summary strips out.
- Notice the pattern before you blame the room. People "not remembering the outcome" is not disengagement or poor listening — they were never in the conversation that produced it, and no amount of attention would have fixed that.
