# The IT-business divide

*Dysfunction `#67` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491374789631209474-z2WH) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- Product and IT have "fixed the collaboration": shared ceremonies, embedded engineers, a joint quarterly planning event — and the divide is still there.
- The roadmap belongs to product; the technical decisions belong to IT; the budgets are two budgets, negotiated separately and defended separately.
- Engineers hear what is being built after the commitment is made, and are asked for estimates rather than for what is possible.
- Nobody below the CEO is accountable for the business value of the technology — product owns the promise, IT owns the delivery, and the outcome falls between them.
- Every transformation improves the interface between the two organisations and none of them removes the need for one.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This is **DP1** doing exactly what DP1 does: it separates the people who understand the technology from the people who understand the market, and puts coordination *above* both. The divide is not a relationship failure between a CPO and a CIO — it is the structure they were each given. Two reporting lines, two budgets, two sets of goals, and an integration point that exists only where the lines finally meet: the CEO.

That is why the usual fixes wear off. Shared ceremonies and embedded engineers move information across the boundary faster; they do not move *authority* across it. The variance that matters — a technical constraint that should change the roadmap, a market signal that should change the architecture — arises where product and engineering knowledge meet, but control over the response sits one level up on each side. So the variance is exported upward, arrives at the top as two competing accounts, and gets settled as a negotiation between functions rather than a decision about the work. What a CPO and a CIO cannot integrate separately, a CEO cannot hold alone.

The uncomfortable implication is that this divide is a reliable diagnostic. If it survives your transformation, the transformation changed processes, roles, and rituals but not the design principle. A boundary you keep having to build bridges over is a boundary that should not be there.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — put integration where the complexity actually lives, not above it.**
- Organise around whole pieces of business capability, with the market knowledge and the technical knowledge inside the same unit and jointly accountable for the outcome — **DP2** applied to the divide itself, not a better interface across it.
- Where multi-skilled groups genuinely aren't possible, make the specialists coordinate as **peers** with joint responsibility for shared goals, rather than each reporting up their own line.
- Give that unit one budget and one set of goals. Two budgets guarantee two agendas no matter how well people get along.
- Move the CEO's role from integrator to **boundary rider** — facing outward toward the environment, not adjudicating inward between functions.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Stop framing it as a communication or trust problem in retros and offsites; that framing quietly makes it the people's fault and buys another year of bridges (see [[communication-problems]]).
- Take one initiative and get product and engineering jointly accountable for a single outcome, with a single number — a legible, small demonstration that integration at that level works.
- When a technical constraint should change the roadmap, escalate it as a *joint* recommendation from both sides rather than as an IT objection to a product plan. Arriving as one account rather than two is the whole point.
