# We transformed, our suppliers didn't

*Dysfunction `#76` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7496080318458867712-TDtN) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- The internal transformation worked. Teams are multi-skilled, own their whole slice of the product, and decide for themselves — and then every request that has to leave the building slows to a crawl.
- A change the team could ship in a day sits in a vendor's global backlog, ranked against every other customer's ask by a roadmap committee nobody here has met.
- Support runs through tiers and ticket queues built for volume, so a two-minute conversation between the two engineers who understand the problem takes eleven days and four handoffs to happen — if it happens at all.
- Contracts, SLAs and account managers all assume a customer that specifies up front and signs off at the end; the team works in weeks and needs to renegotiate what it wants as it learns.
- People start explaining the delays as vendor incompetence or bad account management, and the fix on the table is a tougher contract or an escalation path.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

This is a **DP2 organisation operating inside a DP1 ecosystem**, and it is the same mistake as an agile team inside a command-and-control company — just moved out past the org chart, where it is much harder to see. Internally, variance is now controlled where the work happens. At the boundary, it isn't: the moment a piece of work needs something from a supplier, coordination jumps back up a level, into a backlog, a queue, an account manager. The friction people feel is not the vendor being slow. It is two incompatible design principles meeting at an interface, and every such interface is a place where the transformation stops.

Emery and Trist named the underlying condition in 1965: in a **turbulent field**, the environment itself is in motion, and no single organisation can adapt successfully through its own direct actions alone. That is the trap here. Everything inside the boundary was made adaptive; the field the organisation actually lives in was not, and adaptiveness that stops at the boundary is not adaptiveness — it is a well-designed part inside a system that still can't respond. Hjorteland's case is **J. Robins & Sons**, the Australian shoe manufacturer that transformed itself into the last large domestic producer, competing with Asian manufacture without offshoring jobs. Its suppliers stayed optimised for the volume customers that had already left. The ecosystem destabilised, and the company went down with it — not for want of an internal redesign, but because the redesign had nowhere to connect to.

**de Guerre and Aughton** identify the missing link: ecosystem members remain separate organisations, and their relationships do not become adaptive by goodwill or proximity alone. Being co-located, being a good partner, having a strong relationship with the account team — none of this changes the design principle governing the interface. Nothing in a supplier's structure obliges it to become adaptive on your behalf, and no amount of relationship warmth substitutes for a jointly-designed way of working. The relationship has to be designed as deliberately as the teams were.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — the boundary of the redesign is the ecosystem, not the company. The internal transformation was the right move; it was not the last one.**
- Treat the vendor interface as part of the system you design, not as a commercial arrangement you administer. Map where a decision leaves the team and re-enters someone else's queue — those points are the design work.
- Do the design work *jointly*, with the people who do the work on both sides in the same room. This is domain-level participative work — a search-conference-style session across the organisations, not a QBR where two account teams present slides at each other.
- Redesign the interface so variance is handled where it arises: named engineers who talk directly, shared boards, joint on-call — instead of tiers, ticket volumes and a backlog owned above both parties.
- Make adaptiveness a selection and contracting criterion. Buy for the ability to change together — cadence, direct access, joint discovery — rather than for a fixed scope, a fixed price and a penalty clause, which purchase a DP1 relationship no matter what the supplier's own culture is like.
- Accept that some partners cannot follow, and treat that as a strategic fact rather than a supplier-management problem. A critical dependency that structurally cannot adapt is a risk to the whole system, not a line item.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Find the one interface that hurts most and get a single direct channel opened for it — two named people who may talk without a ticket. It is a small, sellable ask and it demonstrates the pattern better than any argument.
- Use contract-renewal and vendor-review moments as the opening; they are the rare occasions when the interface is officially up for discussion and someone with authority is already in the room.
- Reframe the complaint before it hardens into blame: your suppliers are not failing, they are doing exactly what their structure and their other customers require of them. "Their design doesn't fit ours" is a solvable statement; "they're unresponsive" is not.
- Measure and show the boundary cost — how much of a typical piece of work is spent waiting outside the building. Teams usually feel this and can't evidence it, and unevidenced friction gets attributed to people.
