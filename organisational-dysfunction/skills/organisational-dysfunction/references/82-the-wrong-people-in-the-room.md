# The wrong people in the room

*Dysfunction `#82` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7494491292408885248-HTM0/) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A workshop is called to look at how work actually flows through the organisation. The invite list is built from the org chart: directors, senior leads, an IT representative, the project managers.
- The people who live the flow — who meet the broken handoff on a Tuesday, who invented the spreadsheet that patches it — are not in the room. Nobody questions this; the list looks obviously right.
- Attendees speak *for* their function rather than *from* their week. Contributions arrive as position and summary, not as the specific thing that went wrong last month.
- The workshop produces a redesign of a process that no one present performs. It is coherent, well-argued, and describes the planned system rather than the operating one.
- The workarounds never surface, because the only people who know them weren't invited — so the new design quietly inherits every problem the old one had.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

The room was assembled by the wrong principle. Selecting on formal position and recognised expertise produces a room that **mirrors the hierarchy**, and a mirror of the hierarchy can only tell you what the hierarchy already believes. That is not an oversight in the invite list; it is DP1 behaving exactly as designed. In a structure where knowledge of the work is supposed to be integrated one level above it, inviting the integrators *is* the rational move — you go to the people whose job is to hold the overview. The catch is that the overview is an abstraction of the work, and the thing the workshop was convened to fix lives precisely in the gap between the abstraction and the work.

OST's own formats select differently. The Search Conference and the Participative Design Workshop are built around getting a **deep slice of the whole system** into one room — across functions, across levels, and above all across daily experience — because the system's real behaviour is distributed across the people who operate it, not concentrated at the top of it. The design principle is not "get the decision-makers"; it is "get the system present enough to see itself".

The construct that makes this non-negotiable is **personal capacity**: each person carries a piece of the jigsaw that the group needs, and no one carries it for anyone else. A director cannot bring the frontline's piece, however experienced or well-intentioned — what they can bring is a report about it, which is a different and much thinner object. This is why the room "fills with representatives": once you invite by title, everyone present is standing in for something rather than contributing what only they hold. The variances that actually degrade the system — the ones absorbed daily by improvisation and never escalated — are held by exactly the people the selection principle screened out. The result is a redesign built from the planned system, which is why so little changes when it lands.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — participation is a selection principle, not a courtesy, and the wrong principle produces the wrong system every time.**
- Select for a deep slice of the system, not a layer of it: the people who do the work, across the functions the work crosses, alongside whoever holds the constraints. Cross-section first, seniority second.
- Let the people who do the work redesign the work. A Participative Design Workshop puts the analysis and the redesign in the hands of those who will live with it — which is also the only way the result carries authority once the room disperses.
- Give the group real standing over its own output. A perfectly selected room that can only recommend upward reproduces the same gap one step later.
- Treat "who is in the room" as a designed property of any org-design intervention, with the same seriousness as the agenda. If the invite list came from the org chart, the intervention has already inherited the org chart's blind spots.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- Change one invite list. Ask for two or three people who do the work daily to be in the next session — as participants, not as a panel invited to present and then leave.
- Ask the question in the room: *who here has done this work in the last month?* It is a mild question, it is answerable, and the silence after it does more than an argument about representation would.
- If you cannot bring the people, bring the variances: collect the actual workarounds, exceptions and broken handoffs beforehand and put them in front of the group as data. It is a poor substitute for personal capacity, but it beats designing from the diagram.
- Be honest about what such a room can decide. A group of representatives can set direction and remove constraints; it cannot see the work. Naming that limit is more useful than letting the output carry unearned confidence.
