# The values on the wall

*Dysfunction `#71` in Trond Hjorteland's ["Organisational Dysfunction of the Day"](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trondhjort_opensystemstheory-sociotechnical-orgdesign-ugcPost-7491031608913899521-1ZTs) series — synthesised through open sociotechnical systems theory; paraphrased, not quoted.*

## How it shows up

- A consultant-facilitated offsite produces five words — Courage, Trust, Impact, Collaboration, Excellence — that are then printed, laminated, framed in reception and added to the slide template.
- Nobody can list them a month later, including several people who were in the room when they were chosen.
- The words are unfalsifiable: no decision the company has ever taken would have come out differently under them, and no proposal has ever been rejected for violating one.
- They are invoked downward — in performance conversations, in onboarding, in a leader's all-hands slide — and essentially never upward, as grounds for challenging a decision from above.
- Meanwhile people plainly do share something: what they will not ship, who they refuse to let carry a problem alone, what they think the work is *for*. None of that is on the wall.

## The sociotechnical diagnosis

Emery's distinction between **values** and **ideals** is doing all the work here, and the exercise collapses it. Values are context-bound preferences — what this group prefers, here, given these conditions; they shift as the conditions do. **Ideals** are ends that can always be approached and never fully reached, and precisely because they are unreachable they can hold across changing conditions. A poster of five nouns is being asked to do an ideal's job while being manufactured as a value — a preference, selected by a few people, at one moment, in a room.

The delivery mechanism is the second fault, and the deeper one. Values that arrive from above carry the **DP1** filter with them, weighted by the position of whoever sent them. "Trust" issued by the layer that also sets your objectives, rates your performance and approves your decisions is not a shared commitment; it is an instruction, and everyone reads it as one. This is why the words feel hollow rather than wrong — the content may be perfectly reasonable, but the channel converts any content into compliance. You cannot hand somebody a value and have it become theirs; the act of handing establishes the relationship that makes it not theirs.

And the thing being substituted for is the one an organisation in a **turbulent environment** actually needs. When the field itself is changing — competitors, regulation, technology, all shifting and interacting faster than any plan absorbs — coordination cannot run on cascaded objectives, because the objectives are stale before they land. Emery's argument is that what makes an organisation adaptive under those conditions is people who share ideals, because ideals let dispersed groups act coherently on situations nobody anticipated centrally. Shared ideals are a **coordination mechanism**, not a culture artefact. Laminating five words neither creates them nor discovers the ones already there — it just occupies the space where the real work of surfacing them would have gone, and leaves the organisation believing that job is done.

## What to do

**The real fix is structural — find the ideals your people already hold and give them somewhere to bind, instead of selecting words on their behalf.**
- Run the discovery with the people, not for them: a Search Conference-style process where the group works out what it is actually trying to move toward, in its own language, and owns the output as written. If the output can be curated afterwards by a smaller group, it was values-selection with extra steps.
- Test any candidate ideal against decisions, not against sentiment: name three real choices the organisation has recently made that it would have made differently, and be willing to live with that. An ideal that costs nothing is decoration.
- Give the ideals a route upward. They only become real when someone can use one to refuse a request from above and survive it — which is a question about authority and structure, not about culture.
- Look at where direction is genuinely produced — how work is divided, who controls variance, what gets measured and rewarded — and fix the contradictions there first. A reward system that rates individuals will beat a poster about collaboration every single time, and everyone in the building already knows it.

**If you can't change the structure yet:**
- In your own team, ask what you already refuse to do and what you protect even under pressure. That is a real shared ideal, discovered rather than issued, and it will hold weight the wall never had.
- When the corporate words are invoked, ask concretely what they would mean for the decision in front of you. Either the conversation gets useful or the emptiness becomes visible — both are progress.
- Don't spend energy attacking the poster. It is a symptom, and mocking it costs you credibility with the people who commissioned it in good faith. Aim at the gap between the stated ideal and the structure that contradicts it; that gap is arguable in a way the wording is not.
